I sit, the mat beneath me warm from the night’s chill. The breath comes in, a tiny movement at the tip of the nose, out again, a subtle cooling on the way back. As I note each inhalation I notice a small tug in the throat, a reminder that I would like to reach for the phone. I label the tug “wanting” and let the label rest. The tug softens, the throat is no longer tight, and the breath continues its rhythm. When the next inhalation arrives, the feeling of the air on the nostrils is plain, not “my” breath, not something I own, just a sensation appearing and disappearing. I notice the shoulders dropping a fraction, the weight of the legs pressing into the floor, and the slight ache in the lower back. I name each contact – “pressure,” “stretch,” “ache” – and watch them arise and let go, without trying to hold them or push them away.

In the space between these notes, there is a gap where a story might be told about “me” protecting the body or “I” deciding to answer the phone. The gap remains empty; the sensations come and go, and there is no place for a separate self to step in. The breath does not belong to anyone, the ache does not belong to anyone; they are simply events that appear, linger a moment, and fade. Observing them this way shows that the sense of an owning self is a habit of labeling, not a feature of the experience itself.
