The breath entered as a cool ripple at the nostrils, slipped down the throat, and rose again, each wave already fading as the next arrived. When I followed it, the air felt like a thin sheet moving over the skin of the upper lip, then a soft expansion in the chest that seemed to belong to the ribs rather than to any “me.” As the inhalation paused, the space it left was not empty but filled with a subtle vibration of the diaphragm, a feeling that arose and dissolved without any claim of ownership. The exhalation followed, not as a release of something I possessed, but as a natural thinning of that vibration, a letting‑go that left the body lighter for the next in‑breathe.

The posture was a hinge of bones and flesh: the weight of the sitting mat pressed into the sit‑bones, the thighs rested on the thighs, the spine curved just enough to keep the breath open. I noted the pressure against the thighs, the pull in the lower back, the gentle sway of the shoulders as the breath moved. Each sensation appeared, lingered a moment, and passed, never staying long enough to be clung to. In observing these movements, there was no sense of a separate observer standing apart; the noticing was itself a part of the shifting sensations, a thread woven through the breath and the body without a knot of self to hold it.
