The breath comes and goes, and the throat sometimes narrows. I can hear the faint click of cartilage, feel a subtle grip that appears for a few breaths and then eases. It is not a single, permanent block; it shifts, sometimes moving up into the jaw, sometimes staying light at the back of the tongue. I no longer label the sensation “desire” because the feeling does not carry that meaning for me any more—it is just the ordinary work of muscle fibers responding to the rhythm of air. When the tightening is present, my attention rests on it without trying to pull it apart; I note the temperature of the air, the vibration of the voice‑box, the way the shoulders relax a fraction when the throat loosens.

The pattern is irregular. One inhalation may be smooth, the next carries a brief constriction that lingers into the exhale, and later the tension migrates to the jaw or the neck. Observing does not dissolve it, but it allows the tension to appear and disappear of its own accord, like a wave that rises and falls. The story I once told myself—tension arises, I see it, I let it go—was a useful map, yet the actual experience resists that tidy route. I stay with the moment, noting the rise and fall without insisting on a final release.
