The breath arrived at the tip of the nose, a thin cool current that rose and fell without instruction. When the inhale slipped shallow, the muscles along the spine tightened just enough to be felt, a subtle tension that spread like a thin sheet across the back. The body pressed into the floor, the weight of the legs grounding the sitting, and each exhale released a faint sigh that softened the tightness for a heartbeat before the next breath reinstated it. The mind, meanwhile, hovered between two states: a loosening where thoughts drifted past like clouds, and a gathering when a wish for warmth sparked a quick, bright flash in the chest. That flash lingered only for the length of one breath, then faded, leaving the awareness of the feeling itself rather than the desire attached to it.

In watching the feeling, there was no sense of “my” feeling, no ownership that could be named. The tightening appeared, was noted, and passed, and the mind noted its own movement without claiming it as something it possessed. The sense of self that might have labeled the tension as “my back” or the wish as “my hunger” did not arise; only the phenomena presented themselves, plain and momentary, and the observing presence rested simply with them.
