The breath arrives as a cool tickle at the nostrils, expands the ribs, then leaves as a warm sigh. There is no core “breath‑thing” that stays the same; each inhale is shaped by the air in the room, the tension in the diaphragm, the faint urge to swallow. The exhale depends on the previous inhale, on the slight slackening of the shoulders, on the quiet impulse to let go. Nothing in this cycle carries an independent label that could be pulled out and held apart from the conditions that bring it about.

A tightness in the throat appears just before the mind reaches for the phone. The sensation is not a permanent knot; it rises with the anticipation of a message, fades when the hand stays still, and shifts when a stray thought about the weather drifts in. The feeling is made of the slight constriction of muscles, the memory of past alerts, and the faint background hum of the fan. No essence of “tightness” exists on its own; it is a pattern that appears only when those particular factors coincide.

The act of watching these events also shows dependence. Attention settles on the breath, then on the throat, then on the thought of reaching, each move conditioned by the previous one and by the quiet intention to stay present. When attention wavers, the observed phenomena change or disappear; when it returns, they re‑appear in the same relational web.
