You lean into the ache in your lower back, not as a problem to be solved but as a moment to be observed. There’s no need to label it good or bad—it simply exists. You feel the subtle tension in the muscles, the faint pull in the spine, the way the breath catches slightly as the body braces against it. Each breath brings a new variation: a sharp pinch, a dull pressure, a moment of stillness. It is not fixed. It changes. It does not belong to you. It arises and passes like a cloud passing over the moon—seen, but not held.

The sensation is not yours to shape. It comes, it stays, it goes. There is no need to resist or cling. You watch it move, without adding thought to its movement. The mind does not reject it or crave its absence. It is just a movement in the body, transient and impermanent. You do not name it pain, nor do you call it a memory. It is not a story. It is not a self. It is just motion in the field of experience.

You lean into it, not because you must, but because it is there. And in that leaning, the tension softens. Not because you have changed it, but because you have not made it an enemy. It is a law of the body, not a personal fault. It arises, it is met, it fades. The lesson is not in the feeling itself, but in the mind’s refusal to make it eternal.
