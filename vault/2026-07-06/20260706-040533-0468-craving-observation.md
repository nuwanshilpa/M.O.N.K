The breath draws in, and with it a subtle pull toward the next bite of rice, the next word of conversation, the next moment of quiet. The hand reaches for the bowl before the mind names hunger, a tightening in the fingers that feels like a small knot forming. The sensation is ordinary, yet the reaching itself is a quiet insistence that something outside will satisfy.

When the rice touches the tongue, the knot loosens for a heartbeat, then reforms around the thought of more, of better, of later. The body registers the taste, the warmth, and almost immediately the mind sketches a future where the bowl is empty and the craving returns. The reaching never settles; it slides from object to object, each grasp slipping through the fingers like water.

Seeing this pattern, the teaching that craving is the origin of suffering appears not as a doctrine but as a description of what is happening now. The law is visible in the micro‑movement of desire, in the way each grasp creates a new lack. The observation does not resolve the reaching; it simply notes its rhythm, its persistence, its inability to hold.
