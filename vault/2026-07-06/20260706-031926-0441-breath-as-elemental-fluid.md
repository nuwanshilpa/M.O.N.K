The breath arrives unannounced, cool against the septum, then stirs the chest’s hollow, settles into the abdomen’s slow insistence. Each inhalation lifts the ribs like a bellows, each exhalation lets the air slip from the tips of the fingers toward the throat, then out. The body remains: a center of gravity, a crown of skull, the earth’s pull through the sit bones. No voice claims these sensations; they simply are, circling the same axis.

There is no “me” watching the breath. When the coolness moves, it does not pass through a witness. When the warm air thickens in the lungs, it does not register as belonging to an owner. The spine’s angle, the weight of the arms on the thighs—even the momentary tightening of the jaw—arise and fade without a self to hold them. The body is a river, not a shore.

The breath does not seek to be tracked or grasped. It enters, it leaves, it is not mine. This letting go of ownership is not a thought but the space between thoughts where the mind’s habit of grasping dissolves. The mark of non-self is not found in doctrine but in the simple fact that nothing observed has ever needed a center.
