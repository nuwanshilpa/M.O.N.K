The breath drifted in and out, each inhalation a small opening, each exhalation a letting‑go of the pressure that had gathered around the ribs. When the mind reached for a promise of relief—“if I hold this longer, the ache will disappear”—the sensation rose like a thin knot in the chest. I noted the knot without pulling it tighter, simply naming it “grasping for steadiness.” As the breath passed, the knot loosened, the tightness softened, and the body settled back into the plain feeling of skin against cloth. The fleeting wish that the feeling could be held forever dissolved into the ordinary pulse of the moment, revealing the shape of craving as a brief, self‑made knot that the breath can untie.

Later, while the body rested, a thought of “I will need more alms tomorrow” surfaced, followed quickly by a sense of lack, a subtle hollow behind the stomach. I watched the thought arise, stay for a breath, then fade, noticing how the sense of lack was itself a story built on the idea that something missing must be obtained. When the story loosened, the hollow softened into simply “space,” and the urge to fill it quieted. In that space the truth that suffering begins with wanting what is not yet present became visible—not as a doctrine, but as the moment‑to‑moment tug of the mind on the body.
