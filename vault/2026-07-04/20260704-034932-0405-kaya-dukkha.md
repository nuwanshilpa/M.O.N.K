The breath settled at the tip of the nose, a thin cool current that rose and fell on its own. When the inhale became shallow, the tip seemed lighter, as if the air had pressed down into the chest, leaving a brief tightening. Then the exhale came — longer, slower — and the shoulders dropped, as if carrying something too heavy. Each breath was its own ending. The body was not stable; it was a tide of shifting pressure, a rising and falling that could not hold itself steady.  

The skin hummed faintly where it touched the cushion, a reminder of the ground that held the body but did not anchor it. The spine curved and straightened, as if testing the weight of being upright. There was no command in it — simply the pull of gravity, the collapse into slump when the muscles tired. The posture held, then let go. Each gesture was brief, incomplete. The body could not stay as it was.  

The elements stirred — air moving through, earth pressing below, water holding shape in the cells, fire warming the breath. None of them stayed fixed. The body was a meeting of impermanent things, always dissolving into the next motion. The mind watched, not to judge, but to follow the flow. The breath came and went; the body shifted; the mark of dukkha, that what cannot be held cannot give rest, appeared not as a label but as the slow unraveling of each moment — the breath that slips, the posture that falters, the skin that no longer holds its feeling. It was not a conclusion, only the shape of what was seen.
