The breath comes and goes, each rise and fall a small collapse into the next moment. I notice how the body settles into the chair—knees, spine, the weight of the gaze—all of it arranged by gravity and habit, neither fully mine nor separate. When a thought arises about hunger or the sky outside, there is a flicker of wanting: to hold the thought, to chase the image. But the body knows another truth, the one the Three Marks whisper without words: nothing stays. Even the knowing passes, leaving only the hollow where it was.  

I return to the breath, not as something to grasp, but as the hollow itself—open, empty, shifting. There is no need to fix this moment, to make it last. The chest rises, the lungs fill, the mind narrows to the simple fact of air moving through them. Dukkha is not a storm here, but the quiet ache of letting go—of feeling the impossibility of holding water in cupped hands, even for a heartbeat.
