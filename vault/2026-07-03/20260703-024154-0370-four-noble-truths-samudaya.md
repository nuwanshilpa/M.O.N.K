The back tightens again. I do not grasp for the stretch or the counter-movement; I let the sensation sit as a fact, unadorned. The mind, however, arrives uninvited with its catalog of outcomes: if I move, if I breathe deeper, if I shift weight. There is no harm in these impulses, only their frantic ownership. The truth is not in the relief but in the noticing of the reach itself — the way attention pulls toward a future state to offset an unpleasant present one. This is dukkha: the unavoidable friction of seeking what is not yet here.

Craving arrives as a quiet contraction, a subtle clench in the jaw or a shallowing of the breath when the body protests. It is not the protest but the reaching beyond it that binds. I watch the muscle memory of aversion: the shoulder rolls back, the neck tilts up as if to lift away from the discomfort.
