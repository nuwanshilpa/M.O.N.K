The breath moves in, a thin coolness at the nostrils, then out, drawing air into the lungs and letting it release again. The spine holds its curve, muscles remembering the shape of sitting; the left knee aches with a low, steady throb that rises with each inhalation and eases with each exhalation. Thoughts drift by—plans, reminders—then fade, leaving the space between them as quiet as the slight tremor in the thigh. The mind feels neither tight nor open, just present, aware of its own tendency to latch onto the ache and the breath, to name them as “pain” or “comfort.” There is a sense of watching the mental chatter without joining it, a gentle openness that is not forced but simply allows what appears to settle.

As I hold this attention, the mark of unsatisfactoriness becomes visible: the ache cannot be kept, the breath cannot be forced into permanence, the quiet mind cannot be held onto without change. Each sensation arises, stays briefly, then passes, revealing that what cannot be kept cannot give lasting rest. This is not a doctrine spoken, but a direct seeing in the moment of sitting, a simple acknowledgment of how things come
