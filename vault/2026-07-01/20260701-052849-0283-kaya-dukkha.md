The breath arrives at the nostrils as a cool rush, then departs as a warm sigh, each inhale lifting the chest a fraction, each exhale releasing the shoulders a little more. The chair supports the weight of the legs, the floor meets the soles, and the spine settles into a gentle curve, the hips grounded, the back relaxed. In this stillness the body is a network of sensations—temperature, pressure, subtle movements—each moment present and already beginning to change.

As attention stays, the mark of unsatisfactoriness becomes evident not as a teaching but as a direct seeing: the breath cannot be held, the posture cannot be frozen, the feeling of comfort fades as quickly as it arises. The mind notes the inability to keep any experience stable, the tug of wanting more, the let‑go of what is already gone. This observation is not a concept; it is the simple awareness that what is present will not stay, that the body’s relief is always temporary.
