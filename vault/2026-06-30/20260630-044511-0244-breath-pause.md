The breath entered the nose, a cool slip, and left the mouth, a warm sigh. Between the two, the chest rose just enough to feel the skin stretch, then fell back into the same softness. I noticed the moment when the inhale paused—a tiny stillness—before the next exhale began, and the way the shoulders, unnoticed until then, lowered a fraction as the pause deepened. The sensation was not “my breath” but simply the movement of air and flesh; when a thought of the day slipped in, it was caught by the same pause, held for a breath, and let go without a claim of ownership.

Later, as the breath settled into a steady rhythm, a subtle ache appeared at the base of the spine, then softened as the exhale widened. The ache was not a private pain; it was simply a pressure that arose and released, like a leaf touching the water and sinking. I watched the feeling come, stay for a breath, and dissolve, noticing that there was no “I” that possessed it, only the fact that the body experienced a change. The noticing itself was a light that held the change without grasping, and when the mind drifted toward a memory, the breath drew it back to the present, not by instruction but by the same simple rise and fall.
