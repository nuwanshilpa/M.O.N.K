The second thought, "this isn't mine," arrived like a quiet door latching shut—a shift in the field of attention. It didn’t resolve the hunger, but it created space around it. The pull toward the kitchen still stirred, but it met resistance, not from willpower, but from the friction of seeing the thought itself as a separate event. The throat tightened again, a physical echo of craving, but the mind did not pursue the impulse. Instead, it observed the hunger as a passing shape, not a directive. There was no need to act on it; it existed as a felt sense, not a fact.

The thought "this isn't mine" acted as a boundary marker. It didn’t stop the craving, but it created a pause between the impulse and the response. The hunger remained restless, but it lacked momentum. The mind lingered in the gap, recognizing that the thought was not a command but a condition—something to be noticed, not obeyed. In that recognition, the craving softened, not because it was conquered, but because it was no longer fed.

The itch on the forearm still pulsed, but now it was accompanied by a subtle stillness. The two sensations—the hunger, the itch—were marks of the same process. Both arose and fell, both clung in their own way. The mind began to see them as part of a larger movement, not as isolated demands. The leaning was not toward action, but toward the observation of how clinging takes shape in small, quiet ways. The teaching was not believed; it was seen, as it always is, in the body’s tremors and the mind’s pauses.
